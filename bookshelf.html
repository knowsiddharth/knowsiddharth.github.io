§ 1 — Bookshelf

Things I have read and thought were worth the time. The notes are honest — I am not trying to seem well-read, just recording what actually stayed. Updated when something earns it.

  • Gödel, Escher, Bach
    Gödel, Escher, Bach: An Eternal Golden Braid
    Douglas Hofstadter — 1979
    A book about self-reference, formal systems, and consciousness that disguises itself as a puzzle book. I picked it up expecting something light. It took me four months and reorganised how I think about computation and meaning. The chapters on Gödel alone are worth it — but the whole thing rewards patience.
    ★★★★★
  • The Pragmatic Programmer
    The Pragmatic Programmer
    David Thomas & Andrew Hunt — 1999
    Technically a software engineering book. Actually a book about how to think carefully about problems. I return to it between research projects, not to pick up new tools, but to check whether I am being honest about the ones I already use. Deceptively short, permanently useful.
    ★★★★☆
  • The Art of Doing Science and Engineering
    The Art of Doing Science and Engineering
    Richard Hamming — 1997
    Hamming spent his career at Bell Labs watching great researchers work, and this is his account of what separated them from everyone else. The answer, in short: they worked on important problems. The book sounds obvious until you realise you are not actually doing that. One of the more uncomfortable reads I have had.
    ★★★★★
  • Thinking, Fast and Slow
    Thinking, Fast and Slow
    Daniel Kahneman — 2011
    Required reading before you run your first user study, design your first experiment, or review your first paper. The chapters on overconfidence are the ones I come back to. Relevant to ML in ways that are not immediately obvious but become hard to ignore once you see them.
    ★★★★☆
  • The Innovators
    The Innovators
    Walter Isaacson — 2014
    A history of computing from Ada Lovelace to the internet. What struck me was not the technical breakthroughs but how collaborative the whole story is — almost nothing was done alone, and almost no one gets remembered correctly. Good to read early in a research career, I think.
    ★★★★☆
  • How to Solve It
    How to Solve It
    George Pólya — 1945
    A short book about mathematical problem-solving that applies to almost any kind of problem. My applied mathematics courses keep referencing it implicitly without naming it. I finally read the original and found it gentler and more generous than I expected. The heuristics are obvious in retrospect and invisible before.
    ★★★★★
