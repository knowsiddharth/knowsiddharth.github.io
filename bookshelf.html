§ 1 — Bookshelf

Things I have read and thought were worth the time. The notes are honest — I am not trying to seem well-read, just recording what actually stayed. Updated when something earns it.

  • The ONE Thing
    The ONE Thing
    Gary Keller & Jay Papasan — 2013
    One idea across two hundred pages: find the single most leveraged action available to you and protect time for it. The repetition gets old. But the central question, what is the one thing I can do such that everything else becomes easier or unnecessary, is a genuinely useful thing to ask yourself in the morning. I use it more than I expected to.
    ★★★★☆
  • Atomic Habits
    Atomic Habits
    James Clear — 2018
    The most practically useful book I have read on changing behaviour. Clear pulls together existing research into a clean model and then actually tells you what to do with it. The identity framing is what stuck for me: you are not trying to run a marathon, you are trying to become someone who runs. Sounds like a poster until it works. I came back to this more than once.
    ★★★★★
  • 7 Habits of Highly Effective People
    The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People
    Stephen Covey — 1989
    Older than most productivity books and better than almost all of them. The first three habits are essentially a framework for integrity, and the Eisenhower matrix in Habit 3 alone is worth the read. Some of it has aged into cliche because the ideas got absorbed into the culture. Reading the original reminds you they were sharp before everyone started repeating them.
    ★★★★☆
  • Meditations
    Meditations
    Marcus Aurelius — c. 170–180 AD
    Private notes a Roman emperor wrote to himself. Never meant to be published, which is why they read differently from everything else. No performance, no audience. Just someone who ran an empire trying, repeatedly and not always successfully, to think clearly about how to live. The recurring themes are control, impermanence, and doing your work without needing it noticed. Two thousand years old and it still sounds like something you needed to hear this morning.
    ★★★★★
  • Rich Dad Poor Dad
    Rich Dad Poor Dad
    Robert Kiyosaki — 1997
    Not really a finance book. It is more an argument about the mental model you carry around money, and Kiyosaki is upfront that the accounting is thin. I read it early enough that the reframe actually landed. The assets versus liabilities distinction sounds trivial on paper. It is less trivial when you look at how people actually spend their lives. Worth reading once.
    ★★★★☆
  • The Rudest Book Ever
    The Rudest Book Ever
    Shwetabh Gangwar — 2019
    Blunt in a way that is mostly useful rather than just provocative. The core argument is that most unhappiness comes from needing external approval rather than building internal standards. Not a new idea, but Gangwar does not soften it the way most people do. Some parts are undergraduate philosophy dressed up aggressively. The parts that land though, land hard. Good for a specific phase in life.
    ★★★☆☆
