Data-Driven Robotic Manipulation with ACT

Collaborators: Nischay Khade, Parth Goyal. Advisor: Prof. Sanjit K. Kaul.

Robotics Imitation Learning LeRobot

Moving beyond pre-programmed motion sequences, we implemented a data-driven policy to achieve high-precision autonomous manipulation. We taught a low-cost 4-DoF OpenManipulator-X arm to pick and place objects from just 50 teleoperated demonstrations using Action Chunking Transformers (ACT) and Hugging Face's LeRobot framework.

§ 1 — The Setup

Traditional imitation learning approaches often suffer from compounding errors and temporal inconsistency, producing jerky, unreliable motions. We utilized the ACT architecture, which solves this by predicting a sequence (or "chunk") of future actions at each timestep rather than a single action.

Our hardware setup featured a single OpenManipulator-X arm equipped with a wrist-mounted Intel RealSense D455 camera, alongside a static front-view camera. We collected data using a master-slave teleoperation strategy, recording synchronized visual and proprioceptive streams at 30 Hz for 50 demonstration episodes.

§ 2 — The Challenge of Vision

During our initial deployments, the trained policy achieved a mere 30% success rate. We discovered that the system exhibited significant instability due to visual ambiguity. The robot frequently misinterpreted harsh shadows cast by its own gripper as the target object, leading to "false positive" grasp attempts on the table surface.

This failure highlighted a critical reality of end-to-end visuomotor policies: they are acutely sensitive to the signal-to-noise ratio in their visual inputs.

§ 3 — Environment Engineering

Instead of scaling up the model size or data volume, we solved this through systematic environment engineering. We isolated the workspace with panels, applied uniform lighting to eliminate shadows, used a dark surface for contrast, and wrapped the gripper jaws in red tape to provide a strong visual anchor for the policy.

These physical optimizations increased the signal-to-noise ratio dramatically. When retrained, the policy achieved a 90% success rate—a 3× improvement—demonstrating consistent and reliable grasping across randomized object positions.
